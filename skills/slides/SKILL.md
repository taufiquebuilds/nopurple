# nopurple-slides

AI-made decks are recognisable from across the room: a purple-to-blue gradient, a frosted card, a centred title with a rocket emoji, six bullets of "leveraged synergies", and a "Thank you! Questions?" slide at the end. They look busy and say nothing. People stop reading at slide two.

This skill makes decks that look like a good designer and a sharp writer made them. The writing matters more than the styling: most AI slop is a writing problem wearing a gradient.

## The rules

These are the rules behind every step below. When something isn't covered, decide by them.

1. **One slide, one point, and the title says it.** The title is a full sentence with the takeaway: "We kept 94% of clients, up from 81%", not "Client Retention". Someone reading only the titles should get the whole story.
2. **Show the number, not the adjective.** Replace "significant growth" with "+13 points". If you don't have the number, leave a visible `[?]` placeholder and tell the user. Never invent data.
3. **Fewer words.** Under 40 words per slide is a good default. Detail goes in speaker notes, not on the slide.
4. **Choose a layout for what the slide has to do**, from `references/layouts.md`. Use a big number, a comparison, a chart or a statement, not bullets by default.
5. **Use one theme, one font family and one accent colour**, and keep the accent for the thing that matters on each slide.
6. **Keep charts honest.** Bars start at zero and heights match the values. Label values directly, highlight one bar, and use no 3D.
7. **End on the ask**: the decision, the next step or the date. Not "Thank you".
8. **Look at it.** Render the slides and check them as images before you call the deck done.

## Workflow

### 1. Get the story

Find out, from the request or the material, three things:

- **Who is watching**, and what they already know.
- **The one thing the deck must achieve**: a decision, approval, understanding or a sale.
- **The real facts**: numbers, names, dates and quotes.

If the user gave notes, a document or data, read all of it. If a key fact is missing, ask once. If they want you to go ahead anyway, use `[?]` placeholders, never made-up numbers or quotes.

### 2. Write the headlines first

Before any design, write the deck as a list of slide titles only. Each one is a sentence that makes one point, in order. Read them as a paragraph: they should tell the story with no slides at all. Lead with the answer. Busy readers decide in the first two slides.

Aim for about one slide per minute of talk, and at most 12 for a 10-minute slot. Cut anything that doesn't serve the one thing the deck must achieve. See `references/writing.md` for the headline formula and before/after rewrites.

### 3. Pick a layout for each slide

Use the catalog in `references/layouts.md`: title, statement, section, number, kpis, bars, compare, steps, quote, image and closing. Choose by the slide's job:

- A single fact: **number**
- Several results at once (a monthly report): **kpis**
- A change over time: **bars**
- Two options, or before and after: **compare**
- A plan: **steps**
- Proof from a person: **quote**
- One strong idea: **statement**

Bullets are the last resort, and then 3 to 5 short ones.

### 4. Build it

**Default: HTML deck.** Copy `templates/deck.html` and replace the example slides. Every word, number, name and date in the template is sample content ("Acme Studio", "Approve by 15 November"…): replace all of it with the user's facts or `[?]`. Copy the file byte for byte (for example `cp`), so the CSS stays intact. It's one self-contained file with 6 themes (`paper`, `ink`, `swiss`, `forest`, `sand`, `mono`), set with `data-theme` on `<html>`. Pick the theme from the audience and brand: `paper` or `swiss` for business, `ink` for tech and evening talks, `sand` for editorial or finance, `forest` for sustainability or health. Match a brand colour by changing `--accent` only. See `references/design.md`.

**If the user needs PowerPoint, Keynote or Google Slides,** follow `references/pptx.md`. It builds a real `.pptx` with the same layouts and theme. Google Slides and Keynote both open `.pptx`.

Put supporting detail in speaker notes (`<aside class="notes">` in HTML, or the notes field in PowerPoint), not on the slide.

### 5. Render and look

```sh
python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/render.py deck.html
```

This writes `slide-01.png`, `slide-02.png` and so on, plus a PDF, next to the deck. It needs Chrome, Chromium or Edge. **Open and look at every slide image.** Check for:

- **Text that overflows** or wraps badly.
- **A headline over three lines.** Shorten it.
- **A slide with too much on it.** Split it.
- **Accent colour on more than one thing** per slide.
- **Chart bars that don't match the numbers.**
- **Low contrast**, especially on the `ink` theme.

Fix and render again. This step catches what reading the HTML can't.

### 6. Check for slop

```sh
python3 <skill-dir>/scripts/slop_check.py deck.html
```

It works on `.html`, `.pptx` and `.md` decks and flags: topic titles, emoji, more than 5 bullets, more than 50 words, hype words ("leverage", "unlock", "seamless"…), vague claims with no number, "Thank you" endings, purple gradients and too many fonts. Fix what it flags until the score is 85 or higher. A flag can be wrong, for example a quote that really does use the word "journey". Use judgment, but say so.

### 7. Hand over

Tell the user:

1. Where the files are: the deck, the PNGs and the PDF.
2. The headline storyline, as a short numbered list, so they can check the argument in 10 seconds.
3. Every `[?]` placeholder and any number you need them to confirm.
4. The slop check score.

## Improving an existing deck

When the user shares a deck (`.pptx`, `.html`, `.md`, or pasted text):

1. Run `slop_check.py` on it and show the score and the top issues.
2. Rewrite the titles as sentences, and cut or move text to notes.
3. Choose layouts and rebuild it in the template, or in `.pptx` if that's what they use.
4. Render, look and check again. Show the before and after scores.

Keep their facts exactly. Change the presentation, not the content, unless they ask.

## Things that mark a deck as AI-made

Avoid all of these. `references/design.md` explains why each one fails and what to do instead.

- Purple, violet or blue-to-pink gradient backgrounds
- Frosted-glass cards, glow and drop shadows on text
- Everything centred
- Emoji as bullet points or in titles
- Title Case Headlines That Name a Topic
- "Agenda", "Overview" and "Key Highlights" slides
- Clip-art icons beside every bullet
- 3D charts, and pie charts with more than three slices
- Hype words: unlock, leverage, synergy, seamless, robust, journey, elevate, empower, game-changing, cutting-edge
- A "Thank you! Questions?" final slide
